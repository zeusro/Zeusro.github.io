A brief introduction to the consumer electronics that accompanied the growth of Chinese Millennials, plus my personal experiences using them.

## Film Camera

![Film Camera](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/VINTAGE-KODAK-ADVANTIX-35MM-CAMERA-FRONT_cb18c49c-df49-4708-a1ed-f62cfaf90be5.webp)

In the 1990s, cameras needed film. Film was a brown strip of celluloid connected into a "roll." You had to pull a little film out and fix it in place, then insert two AA batteries into the camera. With each shot, the camera slowly wound the film to the other side. Only after all the negatives were exposed could you remove the film, put it in a light-proof plastic case, and take it to a photo lab for developing.

Kodak was the king of film and cameras back then, and Kodak Gold was one of its classic products. They also donated some old computers to us for teaching purposes.

## Ball Mouse

![Ball Mouse](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/the-satisfying-texture-and-weight-of-a-mouse-ball-v0-nw4avijc8i0e1.webp)

Computers at the time used CRT monitors and ball mice, running Windows 95/98. When booting up, the desktop showed a blue sky with white clouds. Because the ball mouse relied entirely on physical movement, the cursor moved extremely slowly.

## CRT Monitor (Cathode-Ray Tube)

![CRT Monitor](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/crt.jpeg)

The CRT monitor was the only option for computer displays at the time. It was bulky and especially hard on the eyes; after more than two hours, your eyes would feel tired.

## Motorola V998

![Motorola V998](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/V998.jpeg)

This was a phone with only physical buttons. By today's standards it's called a senior phone, but in the 1990s it was a symbol of wealth.

Many young people today may not know that current mobile carriers (China Telecom, China Unicom, China Mobile) all spun off from the postal system. In the early days, you had to go to the post and telecommunications office in person to pay your mobile bill.

Interestingly, during the restructuring some people assigned to China Telecom and China Mobile cried bitterly (in their eyes, it was like losing their iron rice bowl).

The Taiwanese version of Motorola phones had no mainland Pinyin input and used traditional Chinese characters. They had a special feature: after plugging in headphones, you could listen to FM radio (the mainland version probably didn't have this).

## Subor Learning Machine / Gaming Console

![Subor Gaming Console](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/3d21-kcunqzc6739172.jpg)

Talking about Subor games inevitably brings up the Nintendo Famicom (FC/NES). Simply put, the Subor learning machine was a "learning computer" launched by domestic manufacturers in the late 1980s and early 1990s. The keyboard was meant to let ordinary families' children learn typing on a TV when they didn't have a real computer.

But from personal experience, that keyboard was really in the way 😂.

## Walkman

![Walkman](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/Walkman.jpeg)

The Walkman was the carrier of music. Early carriers were cassette tapes, later CDs. The era after the Walkman was MP3 and iPod.

## Game Boy

![Game Boy](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/game-boy.jpeg)

Unlike the Subor console, which needed an AV cable connected to a TV, the Game Boy was a groundbreaking handheld console. I once saw people playing Harvest Moon on it.

## Sega MD Console

![Sega MD Console](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/Sega-Saturn-Console-Set-Mk2.png)

The Sega MD console left the deepest impression on me as a child. It had games like Yu Yu Hakusho: Makyo Toitsusen, Water Margin, and Feng Shen Ying Jie Zhuan. Pirated cartridges cost about 15–30 yuan at the time. The MD console itself had no save system; for games like Feng Shen Ying Jie Zhuan, the save data was stored on the cartridge. If the cartridge's button cell battery died, the save would disappear.

## Sega Saturn

![Sega Saturn](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/Sega-Saturn-JP-Mk1-Console-Set.png)

Compared to the MD, the Saturn was bulkier, and its storage medium shifted to CD-ROMs. I played The Legend of Sword and Fairy on it, and also somehow cleared Langrisser without understanding Japanese.

## "Digivice" 

![Digivice](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/51+SanPlznL._AC_UF894,1000_QL80_.jpg)

This was an electronic accessory sold alongside Digimon. It mainly involved raising a digital pet on a black-and-white screen (essentially the predecessor of QQ Pet).

## Onda MP4 Player

![Onda MP4 Player](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/1284935349_YfWCE0.jpg)

It can be understood as a predecessor of the iPad; you had to import *.avi and *.mp4 files into it via a data cable. I finished watching Inuyasha: The Final Act on an Onda MP4 player.

## Modem

![Modem](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/DSC_0121.webp)

The modem was the "optical modem" of the dial-up internet era. Dial-up internet worked by splitting a line from the telephone line. While online, the phone would be busy; no incoming calls could get through, and you couldn't make outgoing calls either.

## Nokia N72

![Nokia N72](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/n72.png)

Nokia was the final king of the keypad phone era, alongside Motorola at the time. Nokia ran Symbian OS. The domestic mobile internet back then was called Monternet.

## HTC G7

![HTC G7](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/htc-desire_1.webp)

HTC was one of the earliest international phone makers to benefit from Android. The Desire left the deepest impression on me. I memorized the specs of every HTC generation at the time, but unfortunately I never actually bought an HTC phone.

## iPhone 4

![iPhone 4](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/iPhone4.jpeg)

The iPhone 4 was a groundbreaking product from Apple's Steve Jobs era. In my view, Jobs ended the subprime mortgage crisis and created a new trillion-dollar market.

## Mi 6

![Mi 6](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/mi6.png)

When it launched, I waited a whole year and couldn't buy one. I got so angry that I bought a OnePlus instead, and swore I'd never participate in those scammy flash sales again.

## Tmall Magic Box

![Tmall Magic Box](/img/in-post/china-Millennials-CE/z3.png)

The Tmall Magic Box was Alibaba's challenge to the Radio and Television Administration's position. Later, this kind of access module was built into TV systems. The administration kept selling TV boxes to push remote controls (it had the monopoly on TV broadcasting, after all). And Alibaba's Tmall Magic Box, like many other Alibaba projects, was abandoned halfway.

## Conclusion

Consumer electronics after Jobs's death haven't been very interesting. The only things that catch my eye are active noise-canceling headphones, the Switch handheld console, and AI smart speakers.

There's a lack of products that genuinely surprise me.
